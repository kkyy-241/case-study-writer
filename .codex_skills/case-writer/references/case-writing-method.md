# Case Writing Method

## Core Principles Learned From The Source Materials

- A teaching case presents a real decision, challenge, opportunity, or problem faced by a specific protagonist or decision-making group.
- The case should place students in the protagonist's position. It is not a success story, company profile, research report, or consultant memo.
- The strongest cases begin near the decision point, then move backward and forward through background facts needed for analysis.
- A useful case contains ambiguity. At least two alternatives should be defensible, and students should need evidence, assumptions, and theory to argue for a decision.
- Cases are written mainly in past tense and grounded in real facts. Disguised names or data are acceptable only when disclosed.
- A case is not meant to prove that a managerial action was effective or ineffective. Avoid author judgment in the student case.
- Exhibits, tables, timelines, financial figures, ownership charts, market data, and quotations should support analysis. Refer to exhibits in the narrative and number them in order.
- A teaching note is the instructor's roadmap. It should provide synopsis, objectives, audience, assignment questions, analysis, expected student responses, and teaching guidance when needed.
- Before writing, clarify the case's run-specific core focus with the user: protagonist, startup/company, financing event, decision conflict, course, and teaching emphasis.
- Use web search to check the latest important public news before drafting the student case. Integrate material events that affect the entrepreneur, company, financing decision, valuation, regulation, litigation, exit, or survival prospects.

## Case Logic

Use this decision spine:

1. Protagonist: Who must decide?
2. Context: What company, industry, market, and stage make the problem hard?
3. Pressure: What deadline, cash need, investor term, valuation dispute, ownership risk, or strategic threat creates urgency?
4. Alternatives: What real options are available?
5. Evidence: What facts and data allow students to analyze the options?
6. Decision node: What should the protagonist do now?

## Narrative Standards

- Start with a scene: date, place, protagonist, document/email/term sheet/board meeting, and immediate tension.
- Use chronology selectively. Do not write a complete corporate history unless each milestone explains the decision.
- Let quotes and concrete details reveal pressure, incentives, and tradeoffs.
- Translate technical product details into business consequences: cash burn, capital intensity, scalability, market adoption, bargaining power, collateral, risk, information asymmetry, or exit potential.
- Keep the ending open. Return to the protagonist facing the unresolved decision.
- The final paragraph should stop at the decision moment. It should not disclose the recommended answer, teaching interpretation, or definitive outcome.

## Evidence Standards

- Extract exact dates, amounts, valuations, share ratios, financing rounds, investors, terms, revenue, margin, debt, cash flow, production capacity, customer metrics, market size, and policy context when available.
- Build a timeline and a financing table before drafting.
- Build an equity/control table when ownership, voting rights, option pools, limited partnerships, preferred shares, convertible instruments, or founder control are relevant.
- When data is incomplete, state the limitation in the teaching note and design questions that can be answered with reasonable assumptions.

## Common Case Types For This Skill

- Entrepreneurial finance: seed/angel/VC financing, runway, dilution, term sheets, staged financing, investor selection.
- Corporate finance in growth firms: capital structure, cash-flow mismatch, debt capacity, working capital pressure.
- Valuation and negotiation: pre-money/post-money valuation, comparable valuation, DCF/scenario valuation, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, earn-outs or ratchets.
- Control and governance: founder control, voting rights, board seats, veto rights, limited partnership holding platforms, employee option pools.
- M&A finance and exit: acquisition financing, PE co-investment, leverage, exit arrangements, integration risks.
